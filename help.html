Sommerwire help

Sommerwire calculates wire antennas with the method of moments: feed impedance, SWR, gain and radiation pattern, over free space, perfect ground or real soil. It reads and writes MMANA-GAL .maa files. This page explains how to use every part of it, which physical models sit underneath, where they come from, and what is known, uncertain or still untested.

This RF.Guru edition of Sommerwire was made by ON9PK Koen Van den Heuvel and ON6URE Joeri Van Dooren (RF.Guru).

Quick start

  1. The first time you open Sommerwire it shows the 3-element Yagi, already calculated, turning in the 3D editor; after that it reopens the tab you used last.
  2. Pick a model from Examples… at the top, or open your own .maa file with Open .maa.
  3. Press Calculate. The readout strip shows impedance, SWR, gain and the main-lobe angle at the calculation frequency.
  4. Look at the antenna and its currents on the 3D editor tab; drag a wire end and watch the pattern change.
  5. Run a Frequency sweep to see SWR, impedance, gain and the Smith chart over a band or the whole HF range.
  6. Use Optimize to let Sommerwire adjust lengths or positions toward an SWR, gain or F/B goal.
Units: coordinates in metres, wire radius in millimetres, frequency in MHz. Azimuth 0° is the +X axis, counting counter-clockwise seen from above; Z is height.

Approaching a model

A result from Sommerwire answers the question you entered, not the antenna in your garden. Joeri Van Dooren (ON6URE) of RF.Guru has written a series of articles on how to use modelling tools well. In short:

  • Use the simplest model that still holds the physics your decision needs. An array sum or image-theory estimate shows why a pattern looks the way it does; a method-of-moments solver such as Sommerwire is needed when the currents themselves have to be found (bends, closely spaced wires, off-centre feeds, coupling). See Why We Use Simplified Antenna Models—and When We Use NEC and Modeling Antenna Patterns with Python: What the Model Can Prove.
  • Model the system you actually built. A conductor you leave out (coax shield, mast, counterpoise, station wiring) is simply absent from the answer. Keep apart three kinds of error: something left out of the model, a limit of the method, and an input you are not sure of (ground, losses, exact lengths). Sweep the uncertain inputs instead of trusting one number, check that the result does not change when you refine the segments, and compare with a measurement at the same reference plane. See The Limits of NEC: Model the System You Actually Built.
  • The return path is part of the antenna. For end-fed and off-centre-fed antennas the counterpoise, radials and the outside of the coax carry current and change impedance, loss and pattern; a low SWR at one point does not prove the return path is right. See The Counterpoise Is Part of the Antenna, Not an Optional Wire.
  • Compare like with like. A pattern scaled to its own maximum hides differences in gain and efficiency; compare antennas in dBi, over the same ground and with the same losses.

The Limitations section lists what Sommerwire itself leaves out.

Using Sommerwire

Examples, opening and exporting

  • Examples… loads a ready model, grouped by band (160, 80, 40 and 20 m, multi-band HF, VHF/UHF). The list covers dipoles and inverted Vs (plain, loaded, trapped, fan and off-centre fed), ladder-line fed multiband dipoles, end-fed wires, wire and tube Yagis, a Moxon, quads, delta and horizontal loops, a hexbeam, a log-periodic, verticals on radials or ground (including a phased 4-square), a magnetic loop, a Beverage receive antenna and a VHF/UHF Yagi pair. Each example has a short description of what it shows. The RF.Guru EFHW16080 (160/80 m) and EFHW8040 (80/40 m) inverted-L examples calculate both band frequencies together and open the pattern in Horizontal + vertical. The RF.Guru EFOC29 example (a 29 m flat top at 8 m through a 4:1 unun, with 12 m of coax shield as its return path up to an ideal choke) does the same at eight band frequencies, 3.6, 7.1, 10.125, 14.175, 18.118, 21.225, 24.94 and 28.5 MHz (80, 40, 30, 20, 17, 15, 12 and 10 m; 30 m is far from matched, SWR about 17.5, as RF.Guru expects a tuner may be needed there); its feed height, coax route, wire radii and soil are assumptions, and its SWRs are model figures, not measurements (see the README for the sources and the assumptions). It sits beside the plain EFOC29 flat-top example, which is unchanged.
  • Open .maa reads an MMANA-GAL file: wires, sources, loads, segmentation, ground, frequency and comment. Sommerwire's own additions (transmission lines, houses) travel in the comment section, so MMANA-GAL still opens the file and simply ignores them.
  • Save .maa saves the model as a .maa file named after the antenna: Chrome and Edge ask where to save it, other browsers download it. When Sommerwire runs inside another page (such as a preview frame) it shows the file text instead, with Download and Copy buttons; in the claude.ai viewer, Download saves it as .maa.txt (that viewer does not allow .maa), so remove the .txt afterwards.
  • Share link copies a link that holds the model as it is now, whether an example, an opened file or your own edits: wires with their expressions (such as R/4), sources, loads, lines, houses, ground, segmentation, wire and insulation settings, band frequencies, the calculation frequency and the pattern view. It holds no results: opening the link in any browser loads that model, editable, and calculates it again exactly as if you had built it there. Nothing is uploaded and there is no server: the model is packed into the part of the link after # (#sw=1.…, versioned), which browsers do not send to the web server. Anyone who has the link can read the whole model, name and comment included, and the link stays in browser history and wherever it is pasted. If copying is blocked (for example inside another page), the link is shown selected for Ctrl+C or ⌘C. A model too large for a link (more than 32 000 characters, or beyond the limits below) is refused with a pointer to Save .maa; share the file instead. A link that is damaged, made by a newer version or beyond the limits (3000 wires, 64 sources, 1000 loads, 200 lines, 8 houses, 16 band frequencies, 0.01–10 000 MHz, about 25 000 segments) is checked and refused before anything is loaded or calculated, with the reason above the tabs, and the page starts with its default example (or keeps the model on screen when the link is pasted into an open tab).

The readout strip

After every calculation the strip at the top shows the frequency, feed impedance R + jX of the first source (after its balun, if it has one), SWR against the reference impedance (computed from that feed impedance, not from the far field), maximum gain (dBi; the largest total-field gain over all directions), F/B (that maximum against the direction 180° away in azimuth at the same elevation), RDF all-sky (a separate figure beside F/B: gain toward the total-field maximum minus the mean gain over the whole visible sky, with the low-angle-noise figure in small print under it; assumptions), the elevation of the maximum and the polarization (what the label means). Four more tiles give component peaks, each searched on its own over the pattern: H peak and V peak (the largest H (φ) and V (θ) gain anywhere: horizon to zenith over ground, the full sphere in free space, each with its own elevation and azimuth), Low-angle 5–30° · DX (the largest H gain and the largest V gain between 5° and 30° elevation, side by side) and High-angle 60–90° (the same two between 60° and 90°), all azimuths. In the two window tiles the component that matches the polarization of this antenna at this frequency is highlighted: H for a result named Mostly H or Horizontal, V for Mostly V or Vertical, and neither for Mixed H+V, which the small print states; the other component always stays on show (how the windows are named and highlighted). The high-angle tile is called NVIS, with the band, only when the frequency lies inside an amateur band from 160 m to 30 m. None of them is derived from Max gain, which stays the total field (H and V power together) toward its own maximum. “DX” and “NVIS” only name the windows: the figures are antenna figures that say how strongly the pattern radiates each component at those elevations, not that a path exists there (the ionosphere is not modelled). A peak on the edge of its window means the component is still larger outside it; a peak at the zenith has no meaningful azimuth. The window tiles read n/a in free space; all four are marked ≈ when the pattern is approximate and dim with the rest of the strip after a model edit. RDF and polarization are marked ≈ when the pattern is approximate and read n/a when no figure could be made; F/B stays as it is. After a model edit the strip is dimmed until the next result, because it still shows the previous model. For a maximum near the zenith, F/B compares two almost overhead directions and stays near 0 dB; the Far field tab has an angle summary for such antennas. For an example that lists several band frequencies (the RF.Guru EFHW16080, EFHW8040 and EFOC29), a Bands row under the strip shows the SWR, R + jX, F/B and RDF all-sky at each of them (F/B and RDF toward that band's own maximum; the selected band is outlined), all solved for the model as it is now; any model edit blanks the row until the next full calculation, and clicking a band makes it the calculation frequency. The status line underneath gives the number of segments and unknowns, the time taken and how many warnings were raised; warnings are listed on the Calculate tab and should be read, because they flag situations where the numbers are not trustworthy.

Geometry tab

The top section holds the antenna name, the design frequency and an editable comment: the comment section of a .maa file is shown there when you open it, and whatever you type is written back on Save .maa.

The model as tables. Every cell accepts an expression with + − * / ( ) and the constants R (one wavelength at the design frequency), I (inch) and F (foot), for example R/4 or 33*F.

  • Wires: begin point X1 Y1 Z1, end point X2 Y2 Z2, radius in mm, and Seg (segmentation, see Segmentation). Wires are connected only where end points coincide (within 0.01 mm). A wire end at Z = 0 over ground is connected to the ground.
  • Sources: a voltage source in a gap. Position w3c is wire 3 centre, w3b its begin, w3e its end. Phase and voltage matter when there are several sources (phased arrays). Impedance and SWR in the readout are those of the first source; with several sources, or a balun, the Calculate tab lists every source with its own impedance and SWR. Balun n:1 puts an ideal impedance transformer at the feed point: 4 for a 4:1 unun, 49 for an EFHW's 49:1, 1 or empty for none. The impedance and SWR shown are then those the feed line sees, and the Calculate tab also gives the impedance at the antenna itself. The transformer is lossless; its own loss (often 0.5–1 dB for a 49:1) is not included.
  • Loads: a series L and C (µH, pF), a parallel L–C trap, or a fixed R + jX. Placed like sources. Q is the coil's quality factor (its loss), Q C the capacitor's (empty = lossless; a few hundred for a coax-stub capacitor, 1000 or more for a good mica or vacuum one), and Cp the coil's own self-capacitance across its turns (a few pF for a typical air-wound coil), which makes a loading coil self-resonant.
  • Transmission lines: a line between two feed points, with Z0, velocity factor, length (empty = the straight distance) and loss: the matched loss in dB per 100 m from the cable's data sheet, and the frequency it is given at (it is scaled to other frequencies with √f, as skin-effect loss is). Typical values at 10 MHz: RG-58 about 4.5, RG-213 about 2, Aircell 7 about 2, 450 Ω ladder line about 0.3 dB/100 m. Each end is connected across the gap at that point, as NEC's TL card does. Put a source on one end to feed through the line. Reversed crosses the conductors at one end (a half twist). The line itself neither radiates nor picks up anything, which suits coax; currents on a coax shield must be drawn as a wire.
  • Buildings: a house with footprint (centre X, Y, length along the ridge, width, rotation), wall and ridge height, roof (metal grid or not metal) with mesh size, rain gutters (metal or none), downpipes (0, 2 at opposite corners, or 4) and the earth resistance of each downpipe. See Buildings.

3D editor

  • Select (S): click a wire or an end point to edit it in the panel on the right; drag to move it in the work plane. Connected ends move together, so joints stay intact.
  • Draw (D): click to start a wire and click again to end it. Clicking on a wire end connects to it; clicking along a wire splits it there (centre feeds and loads stay where they were). Esc stops drawing.
  • Rotate (R): drag to turn the view; the wheel or a pinch zooms. The view buttons give 3D, Top (X–Y), Front (X–Z) and Side (Y–Z).
  • Work plane: the plane new points are placed in (X–Y horizontal, or a vertical plane), at the height or offset you set, with an optional grid snap and step. New wire radius sets the radius of drawn wires.
  • Segments shows segment boundaries; Currents colours each segment from blue (low) to red (high) after a calculation; Far field draws the 3D lobe around the antenna, and Far-field view chooses what it shows: the total field, H only, V only, or H + V (both surfaces together, H orange and V green as on the 2D plots). Every view uses one scale and one reference, 0 dB = the total-field maximum and −40 dB at the centre, so the shape of each component can be compared with the other and with the total; the line under the view says which is drawn. Two small plots show azimuth and elevation of the latest result in the same view. These are views of one result: switching between them never recalculates, and the impedance and SWR stay the same.
  • While you drag, the model is recalculated live (one calculation at a time; the latest position wins), so you can see the pattern follow your changes.
  • Undo/Redo (⌘Z/Ctrl Z), Del deletes the selection, Fit frames the model, Add house places a house beside the antenna.
  • Auto-rotate turns the model slowly. It stops while you move the mouse over the view, while you draw, drag or type in a field, and starts again 5 s after you stop; untick it to keep the view still (it starts off if your system asks for reduced motion).
  • Transmission lines are drawn dashed; house walls dashed and thin (they are not solved), metal roofs and drains as thin wires.

Calculate tab

  • Frequency: the calculation frequency (the design frequency on the Geometry tab only sets λ for the R constant and segmentation defaults).
  • Ground: free space, perfect ground, or real ground with relative permittivity εr and conductivity (mS/m). Typical values: average ground εr 13, 5 mS/m; poor/dry or city ground εr 5, 1 mS/m; very good/wet ground εr 20–30, 10–30 mS/m; sea water εr 81, 5000 mS/m.
  • Add height lifts the whole model, buildings included, above the ground.
  • Impedance over real ground: Sommerfeld (default; for wires above flat, uniform soil, at any height), Complex image (faster; close to Sommerfeld only above about 0.2 λ), or Perfect mirror (what MININEC and MMANA-GAL do). See Ground models.
  • Wire material: lossless, copper, aluminium, iron (as MMANA, non-magnetic), galvanised steel (magnetic), stainless steel or brass, for the skin-effect loss of every wire.
  • Boom correction: for elements mounted through a metal boom, bonded or insulated, with the boom diameter and its axis; the boom is then left out of the model and the element lengths are taken as built. See Tube elements.
  • Insulation: the thickness of the wire's plastic jacket (0 for bare wire), its relative permittivity εr and loss tangent, and which wires have it (empty for all, or a list such as 1-3, 5). Insulated wire resonates a few percent lower than bare wire of the same length; see Insulated wire.
  • SWR reference: the impedance of the feed line SWR is measured against (50 Ω, or 75 Ω for 75 Ω coax). For a balun or unun, set its ratio on the source (Balun n:1, for example 4 or 49) and keep the reference at the coax impedance; the end-fed, OCF, sky-loop, LPDA and Beverage examples work that way.
  • DM1, DM2, SC: segmentation density, as in MMANA-GAL (see Segmentation).
  • Recalculate on every edit keeps results current while you type.
  • Results keeps one row per calculation so you can compare changes; Clear list empties it.

Far field tab

  • Azimuth and elevation plots in dB relative to the maximum (rings at −3, −6, −10, −20, −30 dB). The azimuth cut is at the elevation of maximum gain; the elevation cut is toward the azimuth of maximum gain.
  • Choose total, horizontal or vertical polarization, Horizontal + vertical (the H and V components of the same azimuth and elevation cuts, both in dB below the total-field maximum, so they share one reference; the elevation title gives the peak of each), or all three. The RF.Guru EFHW inverted-Ls and the RF.Guru EFOC29 open in Horizontal + vertical; every other example keeps the current choice. Hold for comparison keeps the current pattern on the plots as a reference; Clear held removes it.
  • The table under the cuts repeats Max gain, its direction and F/B, and adds an angle summary and RDF (method). Over ground it gives, for all azimuths together, the mean and peak of V and of H at low angles (5–30°) and the mean and peak of the total field at high angles (60–90°). It says “V dominant”, “H dominant”, “high angles favoured” or “low angles favoured” only when the difference is at least 3 dB, and “neither” otherwise. The high-angle figure shows how much the antenna radiates upward, not whether the ionosphere returns it; the note under the table calls it an NVIS candidate only for rows inside an amateur band from 160 m to 30 m (why). A multiband example gets one row per band once the bands are solved. Values marked ≈ rest on an approximate pattern; the note under the table says why.
  • A second table, Polarization: what H and V mean here, gives per band the label (for example Mixed H+V), the H / V split of the radiated power over all directions, the two components toward the maximum and how much of that field is horizontal in space, with the wire geometry stated separately (method).
  • 3D pattern: drag to rotate, wheel or pinch to zoom, sampled every 5°, down to −40 dB.

Frequency sweep

  • Range: a custom range with a number of points; Whole HF, 1.8–30 MHz with dense points inside every amateur band (points per band) and coarse steps in between; 2 m + 70 cm, points per band in both bands and none in between, for dual-band antennas, drawn on a split frequency axis (one panel per band); or one amateur band: the HF bands, and the VHF/UHF bands 6 m, 4 m (Region 1), 2 m, 1.25 m (Region 2), 70 cm, 33 cm (Region 2) and 23 cm. Band edges follow the chosen IARU region (1, 2 or 3); national allocations can be narrower or wider, so check yours.
  • Run sweep spreads the points over all processor cores; Stop ends it early. Find resonance searches for jX = 0 near the calculation frequency and makes that the calculation frequency.
  • Graphs: SWR, R and jX, gain and F/B, with amateur bands shaded, and a Smith chart (hover to read a point; the dashed circle is SWR 2). A table summarises each band: best SWR, SWR at the band edges, impedance and maximum gain and F/B.

Optimize

  • Variables: what may change, with a minimum and maximum. Length, centre fixed (Yagi and dipole elements), Length, begin fixed (legs and radials from a feed point), Move wire along an axis, Move wire end, or a Load value. Moving an end also moves every end touching it. Add all wire lengths adds every wire at once.
  • Goals, each with a weight (0 switches it off) and a target: SWR above the target is penalised; jX is pushed toward zero; gain is rewarded (3 dB per unit of weight); F/B is rewarded up to the target; elevation pulls the main lobe toward a target angle. Several frequencies (comma separated) are averaged, which is how you optimise for a whole band.
  • Start, Stop, then Apply best to model. The graph shows the cost of the best design so far; the table compares start and best per frequency.
  • Gain, F/B and elevation goals need a pattern search at every step, so they are slower.

The models inside

This section says what each part of the calculation does, why it was chosen and what it is based on. Sommerwire was written from the published descriptions of these methods; no code from MMANA-GAL, MININEC or NEC was copied. The sources are listed at the end.

Wires: method of moments (MININEC style)

Each wire is cut into segments. The unknowns are the currents at the nodes between segments, each spread as a pulse over the two half segments around its node; the charge on each segment follows from the change of current (continuity). The electric field along the wire must cancel the source field; that condition is imposed at each node by integrating along the path from the middle of one segment to the middle of the next (point matching). Vector and scalar potentials use the reduced thin-wire kernel: the current is taken on the wire axis and the field point on the wire surface.

  • The kernel integral of e−jkR/R over a piece is done adaptively: midpoint rule far away, 4-point Gauss at medium distance, and close by the static part 1/R exactly (asinh) plus 8-point Gauss on the smooth rest.
  • Where k wires meet there are k − 1 current unknowns (current is conserved at the joint). A wire end on the ground gets its own unknown, so current can flow into the ground.
  • The matrix is solved by complex LU factorisation, reused for each source and transmission-line port.
  • This is the formulation of MININEC (Julian, Logan, Rockway, 1980s), the method MMANA-GAL also uses. NEC-2 uses a different current expansion (sinusoidal interpolation) and an electric-field formulation; for ordinary wire antennas both agree closely, and they differ most for very short segments, sharp angles and closely spaced wires.

Segmentation

Segment lengths follow MMANA-GAL's scheme. DM2 sets the longest segment (λ/DM2); DM1 with SC sets the shortest, λ/(SC·DM1), used near wire ends, corners and feed points where current changes fastest. Seg per wire: -1 tapers both ends (recommended), -2 and -3 taper one end only, 0 uses even segments, a positive number fixes the count. No segment is made shorter than two wire radii. Finer segmentation is usually more accurate (check it by comparing two segmentations) and always slower; the model is limited to 2500 unknowns.

Because segments scale with the wavelength, a long wire that is only there for its influence, such as a mast or a long boom, can need far more segments at VHF/UHF than the antenna itself. When a model would exceed the limit, Sommerwire cuts such long passive wires (longer than one wavelength, with no source, load or transmission line on them) into coarser segments, longest first: about λ/20, and λ/10 if needed. Their current usually changes slowly, so the result hardly moves (for a dual-band 2 m/70 cm Yagi pair on an 18 m mast, λ/20 and λ/10 on the mast gave identical impedance and gain), while the elements keep their fine segmentation. A passive wire close to the antenna does carry induced current, though, so check it: the warning names the wires and their segment counts. Set Seg on those wires to the counts given, then make one wire finer at a time (as far as the 2500-unknown limit allows) and compare impedance and gain; if they hardly change, the coarse segmentation is good enough. Setting Seg yourself overrides the automatic choice. Models that fit are never changed.

How the Sommerfeld ground is computed

For a current element above ground, the reflected field is written with Hertz potentials as integrals over the spectral variable λ of Bessel functions times reflection factors: Rv = (k₁²u₀ − k₀²u₁)/(k₁²u₀ + k₀²u₁) for vertical currents, Rh = (u₀ − u₁)/(u₀ + u₁) for horizontal currents, and a coupling term P = 2(u₀ − u₁)/(k₁²u₀ + k₀²u₁) that turns horizontal currents into vertical fields, with u = √(λ² − k²). Sommerwire splits the reflected field in two:

  1. a quasi-static image: the perfect mirror image scaled by Γ = (εc − 1)/(εc + 1). This part carries the near singularity and is integrated with the same reduced wire kernel as the wires themselves;
  2. a remainder that is only weakly singular. It is computed from the Sommerfeld integrals once per frequency on a grid of distance and angle around the image point (typically 600–2000 points, 20–70 ms), multiplied by R′ejkR′ so that it varies smoothly, and interpolated while the matrix is filled.

The integrals run along the real λ axis: substitutions remove the square-root singularity at λ = k₀, the subdivision is refined toward it, and the oscillating tail is summed per half period and accelerated with Wynn's ε-algorithm. The integrands are written in a form without cancellation (δ = u₀ − u₁ = (k₁² − k₀²)/(u₀ + u₁)), which matters for wires a few centimetres above the soil. This is the same organisation as NEC-2's ground routine; the formulas were derived here from the half-space boundary conditions, not taken from NEC code.

If any wire touches the ground (a ground-mounted vertical, a downpipe), the whole impedance is solved over the perfect mirror, even when that wire is far from the antenna or not connected to it (the status line names the wires), as MMANA-GAL does, and a warning says so: mixing ground models in one solution is not energy-consistent, and current cannot flow into the soil in the image models. The ground loss of such a connection is then only the load you put at the base. Buried wires are not supported.
Gain with the perfect mirror over real ground. In both cases above (and with Perfect mirror chosen), the currents come from the mirror but the pattern from real-ground reflection. These two do not conserve energy. For wires well above the ground the result is close, but a horizontal dipole at 1 m on 20 m, for example, shows 10.3 dBi while its pattern would hold 1.8 times the power fed in, which is impossible (Sommerfeld gives −0.8 dBi). Sommerwire therefore marks such gains with ≈ and checks the power in the pattern: if it exceeds the input power, every gain is lowered until the two are equal, and a warning gives the amount. The limited gain is still too high, because the soil absorbs power that the mirror does not. Ground-mounted verticals are less affected, but their gain is approximate too. Thanks to Joeri van Dooren for finding this.
Buried radials cannot be modelled. Sommerwire has no model for wires in the soil, so a vertical over buried radials, or over radials lying on the ground, is not calculated correctly:
  • With perfect or real ground, calculation stops if either endpoint is 0.1 mm or more below the plane after adding the model height offset. This includes wires that cross the plane. Endpoints less than 0.1 mm from the plane count as touching. Free space accepts negative Z coordinates.
  • Radials lying on the ground (Z = 0) touch it, so the whole model is solved over the perfect mirror. They then behave like a perfect ground screen: no ground loss, too much gain (marked ≈, see above), and a feed resistance that is too low.
  • Changing the number or length of buried radials therefore shows none of the efficiency difference it makes in reality.
Two workable stand-ins: leave the radials out, put the vertical on the ground and add the ground loss as a resistance at its base (as in the 4-square example; roughly 2 Ω for about 120 quarter-wave radials, 5–15 Ω for 16–32, 30 Ω or more for a ground rod alone; these are rules of thumb, not calculations), Raising the radials a few centimetres above the ground is not a substitute: the Sommerfeld ground calculates that correctly, but it is a different antenna, an elevated radial system. For a 40 m quarter-wave vertical with radials 5 cm up, Sommerwire gives only 0.2–0.5 dB more gain for 64 radials than for 4, while Rudy Severns (N6LF) measured several dB (5.8 dB in his setup) between few and many radials lying on the ground (Experimental Determination of Ground System Performance for HF Verticals, QEX, 2009–2010). Use raised radials only to model radials that really are raised. Thanks to Joeri van Dooren for pointing this out.

Far field, gain and F/B

  • The far field is the sum of the radiation of all half segments. Over ground, each contribution is reflected with the Fresnel coefficients for vertical and horizontal polarization at the angle of the ray (space wave). Over perfect ground the reflection is ±1.
  • Gain is in dBi, relative to the net power accepted by all sources together, so wire loss, load loss, line loss and (with Sommerfeld) ground loss lower it; mismatch loss at the transmitter is not included. Power lost to the ground and to the surface wave does not show up in the sky-wave pattern.
  • The maximum is found on a 5° grid and refined down to 0.25°. F/B compares the maximum with the direction 180° away in azimuth at the same elevation.

Low-angle (DX) and high-angle (NVIS) windows

The two window tiles of the readout are antenna figures: the largest H (φ) gain and the largest V (θ) gain inside a fixed range of elevations, all azimuths, each found on its own (the 1° × 4° sky grid of the angle summary, then refined on the pattern to 0.25° without leaving the window). They need a ground and read n/a in free space.

  • Low-angle 5–30° · DX: the elevations a long path usually uses. The name describes the window, nothing more.
  • High-angle 60–90°: the elevations a local or regional skywave path (near-vertical incidence, NVIS) would use. It is not a propagation prediction: the ionosphere is not modelled, so the figure says how much the antenna sends upward and promises no coverage. It is also not ground wave, which travels along the ground and not through this window. 60–90° is the high band the angle summary already used; the tile prints the window so the figure can be read against any other definition.
  • When it is called NVIS. The high-angle tile, and the note under the Far field table, use the name only when the frequency lies inside an amateur band from 160 m to 30 m, band edges included, in the band plan chosen on the Frequency sweep tab. For IARU Region 1 those are 160 m 1.810–2.000, 80 m 3.500–3.800, 60 m 5.3515–5.3665, 40 m 7.000–7.200 and 30 m 10.100–10.150 MHz; Regions 2 and 3 differ at some edges (160 m from 1.800, 80 m to 4.000 or 3.900, 40 m to 7.300, 60 m 5.3305–5.4065 in Region 2), and national allocations differ again, 60 m above all. The frequencies between those bands are not amateur allocations and get no NVIS name, and neither does any band above 30 m. Outside the bands the figures are still shown; only the name is withheld. This is a naming rule for an amateur tool, not a statement about where the ionosphere returns a signal.
  • Highlight. In both tiles the component that matches the polarization label of that same result is highlighted: H for Mostly H or Horizontal, V for Mostly V or Vertical. A Mixed H+V result highlights neither and says so. The label is worked out from the modelled pattern at each frequency, so one antenna can be highlighted differently on two bands; nothing assumes that low angles are vertical or high angles horizontal, and the highlighted component is not necessarily the larger of the two in that window. Both are always shown. Near the zenith θ and φ are both nearly horizontal fields in space and which is called H or V depends only on the azimuth, so read the high-angle pair together.

Polarization: H, V and Mixed H+V

  • H and V are the φ and θ components of the far field, the same two the plots show. They are local spherical components: at low angles V (θ) is close to a vertical field, but toward the zenith θ tilts over, and overhead both are nearly horizontal fields in space; which of the two is larger there depends on the azimuth only. A maximum near the zenith with the larger part in "V" is therefore not vertical polarization.
  • The label is taken from the whole pattern, not from one direction: the radiated power is summed over all directions (linear power weighted by solid angle) for H and for V. Mixed H+V means the two sums are within 3 dB of each other; Mostly H or Mostly V a difference of 3 dB or more; plain Horizontal or Vertical 20 dB or more. The share of each is printed under the label. Mixed does not mean equal in every direction: the split changes with angle, which the H and V cuts and the low-angle columns of the angle summary show.
  • Toward the maximum (the readout tooltip and the table on the Far field tab) the two components are given for that one direction, with the share of that field whose E vector is horizontal in space.
  • Wire geometry is a third, separate thing. An inverted-L has a vertical and a horizontal leg, but all wires carry one coupled current distribution and every field component is the sum over all of them (a horizontal wire also radiates θ off its ends and overhead), so H and V are not assigned to individual wires.

Where SWR comes from

The tuner menu beside Calculate changes the SWR colours in the readout strip, band buttons and sweep’s Best SWR column. It uses the calculated SWR before rounding and leaves the values and optimization goals unchanged.

  • No tuner (default) shows green through 1.5:1, yellow above 1.5:1 through 2.5:1, and red above 2.5:1.
  • Internal tuner shows green below 3:1 and red from 3:1 up.
  • External tuner shows green below 5:1, yellow from 5:1 to below 10:1, and red from 10:1 up.

These generic limits give no guarantee of a match. The range of a particular tuner depends on its design, frequency and the impedance it sees.

The impedance and the SWR come from the feedpoint of one coupled solution of every modelled wire: the vertical and horizontal legs, radials and the return path (for the RF.Guru EFHW and EFOC29 examples the outside of the coax shield up to the choke) are all in the same set of equations, and the source current of that solution gives R + jX. The far field is computed afterwards from the same currents. SWR is never derived from a pattern, a gain or a polarization component, and no polarization view changes it; changing the length of any leg or of the return path does (shortening the EFHW8040 vertical leg, horizontal leg or shield by a fraction of a metre moves the SWR on both 80 and 40 m).

Angle summary and RDF

  • The pattern is sampled over the visible sky every 1° of elevation (horizon to zenith over ground, −90° to 90° in free space) and every 4° of azimuth. Every mean is a mean of linear power gain weighted by solid angle (cos(elevation) per step), never a mean of dB values. V and H are the θ and φ components, as on the plots.
  • Low angles, 5–30°: mean and peak of V and of H over all azimuths. One is called dominant only if the two means differ by 3 dB or more.
  • High angles, 60–90°: mean and peak of the total field. Near the zenith θ and φ are both horizontal fields, and which one a wire excites depends only on the azimuth, so no V/H split is given there. This is the range a near-vertical-incidence (NVIS) path uses; whether such a path exists depends on the frequency and the ionosphere, which Sommerwire does not model, and the name NVIS is used only inside the amateur bands from 160 m to 30 m (below). High or low angles are called favoured only if the two mean total gains differ by 3 dB or more.
  • RDF (receiving directivity factor) = 10·log(gain toward the desired direction / mean gain over the directions noise comes from). The desired direction is the total-field maximum, the direction Max gain refers to and the “Toward” column shows; for an antenna whose maximum is overhead that is the zenith. Two noise assumptions are given, both with noise spread evenly per solid angle over all azimuths and unpolarized (total field):
    • All-sky: noise from the whole visible sky, divided by 4π, the published convention (forward gain minus average gain, i.e. directivity). Over ground this is 3.01 dB above the plain mean over the hemisphere. A short vertical on perfect ground gives 4.77 dB.
    • Low-angle noise (over ground only): noise from 0–30° elevation only, relative to the mean over that band. It is a different quantity and cannot be compared with the all-sky figure in absolute value.
    Losses cancel in RDF, so it describes the shape of the pattern only. Real noise is neither uniform nor known here; RDF ranks patterns under the stated assumption and is not a signal-to-noise prediction.
  • ≈ marks figures from a pattern that is itself approximate: currents solved over the perfect mirror with a real-ground pattern (see above), a main lobe narrower than the sampling grid (the largest sample is more than 1 dB below the refined maximum), or a pattern that holds over 5 % more power than the sources supply.

Wire loss and loads

  • Wire loss uses the skin-effect surface resistance Rs = √(ωμ₀μr/2σ) over the wire circumference: copper 5.8·10⁷, aluminium 3.5·10⁷, brass 1.5·10⁷, iron 1.0·10⁷ (non-magnetic, as MMANA), stainless steel 1.4·10⁶ S/m, all with μr = 1, and galvanised steel 6·10⁶ S/m with μr = 100. For the magnetic steel the internal inductance (equal to Rs per unit length) is included too, which lowers the resonance slightly. The permeability of steel at radio frequencies varies from wire to wire; 100 is a typical value, so treat the steel loss as an estimate: on 20 m a steel dipole loses about 30 times as much as a copper one.
  • A load is a lumped impedance in series at its position. The coil is ZL = ωL/Q + jωL, in parallel with its self-capacitance Cp when one is given; the capacitor is ZC = 1/(ωC·QC) − j/(ωC). A series load is ZL + ZC, a trap ZL·ZC / (ZL + ZC). A fixed R + jX is the same at every frequency.
  • A balun or unun at a source is an ideal impedance transformer: the feed line sees Zantenna/n. It changes nothing in the currents or the pattern.

Insulated wire

A plastic jacket around a wire slows the wave along it, so an insulated dipole resonates lower than a bare one of the same length (or must be cut shorter). Sommerwire models the jacket as a series impedance per metre of wire, the thin-sheath approximation that NEC-4's insulated-wire option also uses:

Z′ = jωμ₀/(2π) · (1 − 1/ε*) · ln(b/a), ε* = εr(1 − j tan δ)

with a the wire radius and b = a + the insulation thickness. The real part of Z′ is the dielectric loss, from the loss tangent. For a 2 mm wire with 0.8 mm of PVC (εr 3.5) the 20 m dipole resonates 2.6 % lower, with polyethylene (εr 2.3) 2.0 % lower, in line with the 2–5 % usually measured. The approximation holds for a jacket thin compared with the wavelength, which is always the case for wire antennas. Typical values: PVC εr 3–3.5, tan δ 0.01–0.05; polyethylene 2.3 and PTFE 2.1, both with tan δ below 0.001.

Transmission lines

Lines are described by their admittance matrix: Y₁₁ = Y₂₂ = coth(γL)/Z0, Y₁₂ = ∓csch(γL)/Z0, with γ = α + jβ, β = k/VF and α the matched loss in nepers per metre (dB/100 m ÷ 868.6, scaled with √(f/floss)). Without loss this is −j cot(βL)/Z0 and ±j csc(βL)/Z0. Z0 is taken as real, which is accurate for HF coax and ladder line. The antenna is solved once with a unit voltage at each line end ("port"); the ports and lines then form a small network that is solved for the port voltages, and the currents follow by superposition. This is how NEC's TL card works. Checked against line theory within about 0.5%.

Tube elements: telescoping sections and the boom

Steps in diameter. A Yagi element made of telescoping tubes is a chain of wires with different radii. NEC-2 is known to be inaccurate there, and the error grows as the segments near the steps get shorter; programs built on NEC-2 therefore replace such an element with an equivalent one of uniform diameter (the method of D. Leeson, W6QHS, Physical Design of Yagi Antennas, ARRL 1992, used by EZNEC among others). Sommerwire's MININEC-style solution does not show that problem, so it calculates the steps directly and applies no such substitution. A 20 m element of 2 × 5.45 m with sections of 32, 25, 20 and 16 mm (centre outward, 1.2, 1.2, 1.5 and 1.55 m per side) gives:

As a rough independent estimate, a transmission-line model of the stepped element with Schelkunoff's average characteristic impedance gives 13.49 MHz, 1% below Sommerwire, so treat stepped elements as accurate to about 1% in frequency until a measurement or NEC-4 says more. The tapered element resonates well above a uniform tube of the same length, which is why telescoping elements must be longer than a uniform-tube design.

The example 3-element Yagi, 20 m, telescoping aluminium tubes on a 50 mm boom uses both: three elements with the same taper (32, 25, 20 and 16 mm), bonded through a 50 mm boom, tuned for a direct 50 Ω feed (SWR 1.3–1.6 across the band, about 12.3 dBi at 15 m, F/B 21–27 dB).

Adding a tube element. On the Geometry tab, Add a tube element builds a straight element along Y from its sections (half-length in metres × diameter in mm, centre outward) at a chosen X position and height: the centre section as one wire across the middle, so the feed point is its centre (c), and every other section as one wire per side.

Elements through a metal boom. An element that passes through a metal boom behaves as if it were shorter, so it must be cut longer than its free-space length. Sommerwire uses DL6WU's measurements as curve-fitted by G3SEK: for a round boom bonded to the element, the total element length is corrected by BC × boom diameter, with

BC = 733·B·(0.055 − B) − 504·B·(0.03 − B), B = boom diameter in wavelengths

and half of that for an element insulated from the boom where it passes through; elements mounted on insulators above the boom need no correction. It depends on the boom diameter only, not on the element, and holds up to a boom of about 0.055 λ. A 20 mm boom at 144 MHz gives 4.4 mm bonded and 2.2 mm insulated; a 50 mm boom on 20 m about 3 mm. Choose Boom correction on the Calculate tab with the boom diameter and the axis it runs along (X in all the examples). The boom is then not drawn; the element lengths you enter are taken as built, through the boom, and every straight element square to the boom is solved shorter by the correction, half at each end. A warning gives the amount. To turn a free-space design into lengths to cut, add the correction to each element. A square boom counts as a round one of about 1.18 times its side.

Buildings

  • Metal roof: replaced by a grid of wires along and across each roof plane, joined at every crossing. The wire radius follows the equal-area rule r = spacing/2π, the usual practice for wire grids that stand for solid sheets; checked against a perfect plane within 1–2 Ω. The mesh should be λ/10 or finer at the highest frequency (1 m suits HF).
  • Gutters: 4 cm radius wires along both eaves, 15 cm out and 10 cm below the roof edge, not bonded to the roof (brackets on a wooden fascia usually make no contact).
  • Downpipes: vertical wires from the gutter ends into the ground, with the earth resistance as a load at the foot (typically tens to hundreds of ohms at HF; 0 means a perfect connection; choose no downpipes if they end in plastic drains). Because they touch the ground, a house with downpipes switches the impedance to the perfect mirror (see the warning above).
  • Walls: drawn for reference only. Stone, brick and wood are dielectrics, which a wire solver cannot model; wiring, a steel frame or reinforcement can be drawn as wires.

Objects near the antenna

A mast, a fence, a gutter, a feed line or another antenna nearby can change impedance and pattern, and how much is hard to guess: it depends on the object's length (close to a resonant length it matters most), its distance, its orientation, whether it is bonded or earthed, and the frequency. There is no general safe distance. To judge an object, calculate the antenna first without it and note the impedance and pattern; then add it and repeat the frequency sweep over the whole band, with a few distances and orientations (a symmetric placement can hide coupling that shows up when the object is turned), and compare the full azimuth pattern, not only one cut. Decide beforehand what change in SWR or gain you accept. Thanks to Joeri van Dooren for this advice.

Optimizer

A trial that makes a wire touch the ground switches the whole model to the approximate perfect mirror, which can make gain look much better without being so; when the start is a proper Sommerfeld calculation, such trials are ruled out, and the status says how many there were. Gains from the perfect mirror are marked ≈ in the optimizer table and the sweep summary.

A bounded Nelder–Mead simplex search: the variables are scaled to their min–max range, and the search restarts with smaller steps around the best point. It runs in its own background thread. Like every local search it can stop in a local optimum; try other start values or narrower ranges if the result is poor.

.maa files

The layout was reconstructed from MMANA-GAL basic files. Sommerwire reads and writes the name, frequency, wires, sources, loads, segmentation and the ground/height/material line. Transmission lines and houses are stored as comment lines starting with Sommerwire TL: and Sommerwire HOUSE:; baluns, the extra load values (Q C, Cp), insulation and the newer materials as Sommerwire SRC:, LOAD:, INS: and MAT: lines. MMANA-GAL ignores these lines, and gets steel and stainless steel as iron and brass as copper. The impedance ground model (Sommerfeld, complex image or perfect mirror) is a Sommerwire setting and is not stored in the file. A parallel trap is written as load type 2; MMANA-GAL may not read it the same way.

Resistance agrees within 1–3% and gain within 0.1 dB; reactance differs by 1–4 Ω, the same offset the two codes show in free space (a known difference between the MININEC and NEC current expansions), and it shrinks with finer segmentation. It has not fully settled everywhere: for the vertical with radials at 0.3 m, Sommerwire's reactance moves from −52.9 to −48.6 Ω between the two segmentations while resistance and gain stay put, so two segmentations show the trend but not the final value. The gains are the peaks of one azimuth cut, and these are comparisons between two programs, not measurements of real antennas.

DX Commander with 16 radials is the exception. With lossless wires in both programs and 0.1 m segments, the feed resistance over average ground differs by 11 Ω on 40 m (nec2c 56.7, Sommerwire 45.6 Ω) and 19 Ω on 20 m (50.7 against 31.5 Ω), and the gain by about 1 dB. A large difference is there in free space (4 and 12 Ω) and over perfect ground (9 and 20 Ω) too, so the cause is the antenna's hub (0.8 mm wires, 6–12 cm long, many junctions), not the ground model. (An earlier version of this comparison gave Sommerwire copper wire and NEC-2 lossless wire, which made the gap look smaller, 10–16 Ω; Joeri van Dooren spotted this.) A power balance over perfect ground with lossless wires is a useful check: the gain integrated over the hemisphere must equal the input power. Sommerwire gives 1.000; nec2c gives 0.81 (40 m) and 0.78 (20 m), against 0.985 for a plain ground plane. This points to a numerical problem in NEC-2 at that junction (NEC-2 is known to be sensitive to short segments and many-wire junctions), but a power balance alone cannot prove that Sommerwire's currents are right, so treat the DX Commander's feed resistance in both programs as uncertain by about 10–20 Ω. The change that real ground adds compared with perfect ground agrees within about 2 Ω between the codes.

Limitations

  • Thin wires only. Wire radius must be much smaller than the segment length and the wavelength. Wires closer together than a few radii, very short segments next to long ones, sharp angles and joints of very different radii are less accurate (as in MININEC and NEC). For elements of stepped tube diameters, expect a resonance uncertainty of about 1 % (see Tube elements).
  • Ground connections. A wire touching the ground forces the perfect mirror for the whole impedance. Buried radials and wires passing through the ground surface are not supported (that needs NEC-4-type integrals); a wire below the surface is refused with an error.
  • Flat, uniform ground only. No layered soil, slopes, hills or nearby water boundaries.
  • No ground wave. The pattern is the sky wave; gain right at the horizon and surface-wave range are not computed.
  • Dielectrics other than wire insulation are not modelled: stone or brick walls, trees, wet wood. Insulated wire is modelled with the thin-sheath approximation.
  • Lines, loads and baluns are simplified. Transmission lines have a real Z0 and do not radiate; line loss is one figure in dB/100 m at one frequency, scaled with √f (conductor loss; dielectric loss, which grows with f, is not separated); common-mode current on a coax shield must be drawn as a wire. Loads are lumped: a trap or coil has no physical size. A balun is an ideal, lossless impedance transformer and does not stop common-mode current; a choke is modelled as an open end or a high-impedance load on the shield wire.
  • Steel: the permeability of galvanised steel is a typical value (μr 100); real wire varies. One material applies to all wires.
  • The readout strip and the sweep use the first source; the Calculate tab lists every source.
  • Model size is limited to 2500 unknowns (long passive wires are coarsened automatically to fit); large house grids at high frequencies are slow.
  • VHF/UHF works the same as HF, but thin-wire rules get tighter: tube elements, booms and mounting clamps are often thick compared with the segment length, and an element passing through a drawn boom without a joint is only an approximation; leave the boom out and use the boom correction instead (see Tube elements). The correction applies only to straight elements square to the boom, for a round metal boom up to about 0.055 λ.
  • Numbers can differ slightly from MMANA-GAL and more from NEC; differences of a few percent in R and a few ohms in X are normal between codes.

Further reading. Every result is a model of an antenna, not the antenna itself. Joeri Van Dooren (ON6URE) explains how to tell a part left out of the model, a limit of the method and an uncertain input apart, and how to check a model against measurements, in The Limits of NEC: Model the System You Actually Built; it applies to Sommerwire as much as to NEC. G. J. Burke's NEC-5 Validation Manual (LLNL-SM-791163, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, 2019, part of NEC 5.0 from LLNL) shows, case by case, where the numerical errors of method-of-moments codes arise and how they shrink with finer segmentation.

Open questions and further investigation

  • Sommerfeld against measurements. The ground now matches NEC-2 (nec2c) closely (see the comparison), but neither has been compared with measured impedances of a real radial field here.
  • Small, crowded junctions such as the DX Commander hub: Sommerwire conserves power there and NEC-2 does not, but a third reference (NEC-4, or a measurement) would confirm which current distribution is right.
  • Buried radials (level 2). Modelling wires in the soil needs the transmitted-field integrals NEC-4 has. Radials a few centimetres above the ground are not a stand-in (see Buried radials). A set of cases comparing raised radials with radials on and in the ground, against NEC-4 or measurements, would show where the two regimes part.
  • Ground-mounted verticals with real ground. Today these fall back to the perfect mirror plus a base load. A proper treatment of a wire entering the soil is open.
  • Hexbeam: a resonance just below 10 m in the tuned model remains unexplained (suspected interaction with the 20 m driver).
  • EFOC29: 80 m SWR is about 3.5–3.8 across 3.5–3.8 MHz, higher than the below 3 usually expected. Likely causes are the unmodelled unun, choke and coax losses and the routing of the coax; the product's exact wire gauge and coil details are unknown.
  • DX Commander file: the 2.5 m element starts 0.05 mm above the hub corner, so in the original file it is not connected. Check whether that matches the real antenna.
  • House model assumptions: gutter bonding, downpipe positions and earth resistance are guesses; measure or vary them to see how sensitive your antenna is.
  • Ground constants: the default (εr 13, 5 mS/m) is an "average" value; real soil varies with moisture and season, and with Sommerfeld the results near the ground depend strongly on it.
  • Accuracy of the Sommerfeld table for very large models (several wavelengths across) and very close wire–image distances has only been spot-checked.

  • The credits at the foot of the app now show two Belgian-style callsign plates, Koen first: ON9PK Koen Van den Heuvel links to his QRZ.com page and ON6URE Joeri Van Dooren to on6ure.be. The plates are drawn by the page itself and load nothing from other sites.
  • The names, roles, credits and licence are unchanged, and the ?embed=1 presentation still shows no footer.

2026-11-28 · Phone layout

  • On a phone the app now opens at the width of the screen instead of as a shrunken desktop page. Up to 640 px wide it uses one column: Examples on its own row, the file and share buttons beside each other, the tuner choice next to Calculate, and the readout in two columns with frequency and SWR first. Nothing was removed.
  • The tab bar stays on screen on a phone: at the bottom until you scroll to it, at the top after. Choosing a tab while it is pinned brings the start of that tab into view. On any screen the arrow keys, Home and End now move between tabs.
  • In the 3D editor on a phone the model comes straight after the mode buttons and is larger. With a finger, dragging empty space turns the view in Select mode, two fingers zoom, and wires and wire ends are easier to hit.
  • Wide tables scroll sideways inside their own frame; the page no longer does. Buttons, fields and tick boxes are larger on a phone, typing in a field no longer makes the browser zoom in, and the Save and Share dialogs fit the screen.
  • Larger screens, share links, the solver and the ?embed=1 presentation are unchanged.

2026-11-28 · Embeddable 3D view, tidier band strip

  • Under the readout, Bands now sits on its own row below the antenna name, and the band buttons line up in equal columns below it, one column on a narrow screen. Each button still shows SWR, impedance, F/B and RDF and still makes its band the calculation frequency.
  • Adding ?embed=1 to the address gives a read-only presentation for another web page, such as a product page: the Bands label, one row of short band pickers (the frequency is in each picker's tooltip) and the turning 3D view. A picker calculates that band and updates the view. It opens the RF.Guru EFOC29, or the model of a share link when ?embed=1 is put before its #sw=…. It uses no browser storage and exchanges no messages with the page around it. The README describes a ready-to-use iframe.
  • Share links, their format and the full app are unchanged.

2026-11-28 · One theme, EFOC29 first

  • RF.Guru is the only theme: the Theme switch and the Koen (original) look are gone, and a theme choice stored by an earlier visit is ignored and cleared. The app and this Help page are always dark, with RF.Guru gold for actions and links.
  • An ordinary visit opens the RF.Guru EFOC29 example with its eight bands, the same model as in the Examples menu, and calculates it. A share link, an opened .maa file or an example you pick still replaces it.
  • The version number under the name and in the footer is gone; these dated notes record the changes. The #sw=1.… share links are unchanged.

2026-11-28 · Themes

  • Two themes, chosen with Theme at the top right. RF.Guru is the default in this edition: dark, with RF.Guru gold for actions and links, the Sommerwire mark in RF.Guru colours at the top left and a link to the RF.Guru edition on GitHub. Koen (original) is the original Sommerwire look, which follows your system's light or dark setting.
  • The browser remembers the choice, for the app and for this Help page. If it blocks site storage, the theme changes for the open page only and the next visit starts in RF.Guru.
  • Only the look changes: models, results, share links and saved .maa files are the same in both themes.

2026-11-28 · Model sharing and staged results

  • Share link copies a link that holds the editable model; opening it loads and calculates that model. Model sharing
  • The SWR colours follow a tuner choice: no tuner, internal tuner or external tuner. Tuner colours
  • The low-angle (5–30°) and high-angle (60–90°) readouts show the H and V gain side by side. Angle windows
  • Impedance and SWR appear before the far-field pattern finishes, and repeated calculations are faster. Staged results
